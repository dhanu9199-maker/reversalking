Why RRK Waits for the Close: The Anatomy of a Rejection-With-Reclaim Bar

Intrabar touches are noise; closed-bar structure is information. A bar-by-bar breakdown of the exact conditions RRK requires before it will mark anything at all.

Every indicator author faces the same fork: react to what is happening inside the bar, or wait for the bar to finish and commit to what happened. RRK waits. This article walks through exactly what the engine checks, bar by bar, and why each condition exists.

The cost of not waiting

An intrabar trigger sees price press the upper band and fires immediately. What it cannot see is the rest of the bar: buyers stepping back in, the close recovering inside the channel, the extreme of the prior bar reclaimed in a single session. Intrabar logic also re-fires while price lingers at the edge — five triggers where one structure exists.

Waiting for the close costs you immediacy. It buys you definition: when the marker prints, the entire sequence already happened, and every chart reloaded tomorrow will agree on that history. Repainting arguments end before they start.

The four conditions, in order

Take an upper-band RS candidate. On the current closed bar, all of the following must be true:

1. Yesterday pressed the edge. The previous bar’s high reached the channel top (within half a tick). Without the press there is no setup — price merely being elevated near the band is a different, weaker observation. This is the “touch ≠ signal” principle encoded as code: contact is necessary but never sufficient.

2. Today closed bearish and lower. The bar must close red AND below yesterday’s close. A doji that closes flat at the highs tells you nothing about rejection; this condition demands that sellers actually took control of the session, not just visited it.

3. Today closed beyond yesterday’s low. This is the reclaim test and the strictest cut. Closing under the prior bar’s low means the reversal bar overwhelmed the entire prior session’s range — the failed breakout trapped whoever bought the press. Structures that pass conditions 1–2 but fail #3 are indecision, and RRK stays silent on them.

4. Context says pullback, not collapse. Two long moving averages arbitrate: the slow RMA(600) must still sit below price, and the medium RMA(400) must still sit below the fast trend line. In plain terms: the larger trend frame has to support “healthy retest of an extreme” rather than “distribution beginning.” A perfect-looking rejection during a structural breakdown fails here — by design.

All four true on one bar → RS prints once. The once-per-edge guard then disarms that band until the channel itself extends (a genuinely new extreme), and a cooldown keeps the engine quiet for a minimum stretch afterward. RB is the exact mirror at the lower band.

Why so few markers survive

Run the arithmetic mentally: condition 1 eliminates most bars outright; condition 3 eliminates most survivors; condition 4 removes the tempting-but-broken leftovers. What remains is deliberately scarce. Scarcity is not a limitation of the engine — it is the output. An overlay that marks nothing is doing its job on most charts most days; its silence is information too.

What a marker does and does not tell you

It tells you: a complete, precisely-defined rejection-with-reclaim structure printed at a cycle extreme within a supportive context. Past tense, verifiable, identical on every user’s chart.

It does not tell you: what happens next, how far price will travel, where your stop belongs, whether the trade fits your risk budget. Those belong to your process. RRK’s job ends at the description; yours begins there.
