Donchian Channel Settings: What Changing the Length Really Does

The length parameter is not a tuning knob for better results — it is a choice about what the word "extreme" means on your chart. What N controls mechanically, and why RRK commits to 1,400 bars.

Changing a Donchian channel’s length does not make it more accurate — the channel is always accurate, in the sense that it always shows exactly the highest high and lowest low of the last N bars. What changing N changes is the definition of an extreme: which price events qualify as touching the edge, how often edges get tested, and how much history a test is measured against. That is a design decision, not an optimization, and treating it as one is the root of most settings confusion.

What does the length N actually control?

Mechanically, one thing: the lookback window. Upper band = highest high of the last N bars. Lower band = lowest low of the last N bars. Everything else follows from that window:

  • Shorter N → a new extreme happens more often (less history needed for a record), so the bands update constantly, hug price tightly, and get tested frequently. More markers, more signals, more noise.
  • Longer N → an extreme requires overpowering more history, so the bands sit farther out, move rarely, and mark genuinely rare events. Fewer markers, quieter chart, slower feedback.

The classic 20-period length descends from the Turtle-traders’ four-week breakout system, and most platform defaults echo it. It was chosen for a specific daily-chart trend-following process in the 1980s — not derived as a universal optimum, and no derivation of that kind exists for any N.

Why does RRK use 1,400 bars?

Deliberately, and far outside the usual swing-trading window. On daily charts, 1,400 bars is roughly five and a half years of history, so the upper and lower bands hold the true multi-year cycle extremes — prices few living traders on that chart have pressed. The consequences cascade through the whole engine:

  • Band touches become rare, noteworthy events rather than weekly occurrences. Scarcity is built in at the parameter level.
  • When a marker does print, it references an extreme with real history behind it, not a two-month local high.
  • Slowly-moving bands make the once-per-edge guard coherent: an edge stays “used” until the channel itself extends to a genuinely new extreme.

This is a statement about which cycle RRK wants to describe, made visible in a constant. It is not a claim that 1,400 outperforms other lengths — such claims aren’t measurable in the way marketing implies, and the engine treats them as out of scope.

Should you tune N for your market or timeframe?

You can — every N produces internally consistent, well-defined structures. The honest framing is a matching problem, not a performance problem: pick the N whose “extreme” corresponds to the cycle you actually trade (intraday squeezes, swing ranges, yearly horizons), confirm the resulting event frequency fits your attention budget, then stop adjusting. Two costs of chronic retuning deserve names:

  1. Incomparability — markers printed under different settings describe different structures, so your own chart history stops meaning one thing.
  2. Hindsight drift — settings changed after seeing outcomes quietly convert a descriptive tool into a curve-fit narrative.

This is why the X edition of RRK hard-codes the engine constants entirely: identical definitions on every user’s chart, immune to both costs. The tunable V10 edition exists for study and experimentation, with the same caveat attached.

What about the other Donchian parameters?

Common variants — percentage offsets that push bands slightly beyond the raw extreme, smoothed or averaged bands, midline plots — each redefine the edge again. RRK keeps the raw highest-high/lowest-low construction with no offset, because the rejection-with-reclaim logic depends on the edge meaning the exact prior extreme: the four-condition bar anatomy breaks down why approximate edges would blur the setup. For what happens after an edge fails rather than breaks, see the comparison of Donchian exit approaches.

What this shows, what it doesn’t, and the risks

Shows: the exact N-bar extremes for whatever length is configured — a location map of where price stands relative to its own recent history.

Doesn’t show: any probability, expectation, or ranking of settings by outcome. No N is “best” in a verifiable sense; the parameter defines vocabulary, it doesn’t grade it.

Risks to weigh: short lengths generate constant edge activity that invites overtrading; very long lengths can go months between events, testing patience and sample-size assumptions. Any fixed window misdescribes markets whose cycle character shifts. And the deepest risk is meta: believing a settings search is a search for an edge, when it is only a search for a definition.
