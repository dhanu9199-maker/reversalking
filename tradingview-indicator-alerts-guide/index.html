TradingView Indicator Alerts: A Plain-English Setup Guide

Alerts turn an on-chart tool into a watchtower — but the dialog offers more choices than most traders need and hides one setting that matters more than all the others. A no-code walkthrough of wiring alerts for a custom indicator, and what each option actually does.

Short answer: right-click (or Alt-click) the chart, choose “Add alert,” pick your indicator, select which of its signals to watch, set the trigger condition, and decide where the notification goes. The whole flow takes under a minute — the craft is in knowing which condition to pick, and above all when the alert evaluates.

What do you need before an alert will work?

Two things, both easy to overlook. First, the indicator must be on your chart — TradingView alerts reference what is plotted, so a script with signal conditions but no visible plots gives the alert dialog nothing to attach to. Second, the indicator’s author must have exposed its signals as alert conditions; if they did, each one appears by name in the condition dropdown when you select the indicator. RRK ships four named alert hooks — its two band-edge reversal structures and its two momentum-extreme dots — so every marker the engine can draw is individually subscribable.

One plan note worth knowing: the number of simultaneous price alerts you can hold depends on your TradingView subscription tier, so it is worth checking your allowance before committing slots to minor signals.

How do you actually wire one?

The full path, step by step:

  1. Open the dialog. Right-click anywhere on the chart and choose “Add alert,” or press Alt+A.
  2. Pick the source. In the “Condition” dropdown, switch from the default symbol/price options to the indicator’s name, then choose the specific signal from the second dropdown.
  3. Choose the trigger timing — see the next section. On this single choice hangs everything else.
  4. Set the expiration. “Open-ended” keeps the alert alive until you delete it; dated expiration frees the slot automatically.
  5. Name it deliberately. Six weeks from now, “RRK X · RS structure” is legible and “Alert 7” is not. Include the symbol and timeframe in the name, because notification text does not always carry them.
  6. Choose notification channels. In-app notify, email push, or both. Sound and duration are taste.
  7. Create. The alert appears in the right-panel clock tab; manage or clone it there.

Which trigger option matters most?

“Only Once,” “Once Per Bar,” “Once Per Bar Close,” or “Every Time” — this dropdown is the difference between an alert you trust and one that cries wolf.

  • Only Once fires a single time and deactivates. Good for one-shot level watches; wrong for ongoing monitoring.
  • Once Per Bar can fire during the bar while it is still forming — and then the bar can un-form the situation before closing. For anything that waits for a completed structure, this mode generates false alarms by construction.
  • Once Per Bar Close evaluates only when a bar completes. This is the setting that matches how serious indicators define their own logic.
  • Every Time re-fires on every evaluation tick — appropriate only for genuinely tick-level conditions.

The alignment principle is simple: an alert should evaluate on the same clock as the logic it reports. If an indicator defines its structures on closed bars — as RRK deliberately does, waiting for each candle to finish so history cannot repaint — then “Once Per Bar Close” is the only faithful translation of that definition into a notification. Anything faster reports situations the engine itself would never mark. We explain that closed-bar philosophy in why RRK waits for the close.

What are alerts actually good for?

The underrated use of alerts is not trading triggers — it is attention triage. You cannot watch forty symbols across six timeframes, and you should not try. Alerts invert the workload: instead of scanning charts for interesting moments, defined moments come find you, and your scarce attention goes to judging them.

For a structure-marking overlay, the practical pattern is coverage: subscribe to each signal type once per symbol/timeframe you care about, let silence be the default state, and treat each firing as a prompt to read the chart — context, location, structure — rather than as information complete in itself. An alert says “look here”; it never says “do this.”

A maintenance habit matters too: alerts bind to the indicator version at creation time in some cases, and they silently die if the script is removed from your favorites or the author updates the script ID. Glance at the clock tab weekly; re-create anything stale. And remember the demo-phase reality — access to protected scripts like RRK X is granted per username, so alerts work only on charts where your granted access is active.

What this shows, what it doesn’t, and the risks

What this shows: a timely, mechanical notification that a precisely-defined condition evaluated true on your chosen clock — nothing more interpretive than that.

What it doesn’t show: whether the situation is worth acting on, what size fits your account, or what happens next. The alert is the doorbell, not the guest.

Risks: missed notifications during outages, connection drops, or exhausted alert slots; over-firing if trigger timing is mismatched to the underlying logic; and the standing market risks — leverage, gaps, execution slippage — that no notification system touches. Decisions made on alert firings remain entirely yours.
